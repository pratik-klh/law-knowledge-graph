# Deposition Objections

## Procedure
After the witness is sworn, the noticing party conducts the **direct** examination, then opposing counsel **cross**-examines (to discredit, show inconsistencies, or get admissions). Objections are allowed during both.

## How objections work
- No judge is present, so an objection just **flags the issue** for a later ruling.
- **Types:** form (e.g., leading) vs substance (e.g., privileged); **specific** (states the basis), **general** (just "objection," which preserves little), **continuing** (covers a whole line of questioning). You can also object to nonresponsive answers.
- Testimony is taken **subject to the objection**, and the deponent **answers**.

## Instruction not to answer: only 3 situations (Rule 30(c)(2))
1. To preserve a **privilege**
2. To enforce a **court-ordered limitation**
3. To present a **motion to terminate or limit** the deposition (bad faith, unreasonable annoyance, embarrassment, or oppression)

## Waiver
- Objections to **privilege**, and objections that **could have been cured** at the deposition (typically **form**), are **waived** if not made then.
- Objections to **substance** (e.g., relevance) are preserved for trial.

## Remedies
- If a deponent is wrongly told not to answer, **move to compel**.
- For a bad-faith deposition, **move to limit or terminate**.
- Don't end a deposition early. Re-deposing the same witness needs leave of court (Rule 30(a)(2)).
- **Written depositions:** objections must be served in writing before the response deadline. For recross questions, the objecting party has **7 days**.
