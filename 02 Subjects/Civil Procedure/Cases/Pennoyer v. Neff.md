# Pennoyer v. Neff (1878)

> [!warning] Historical background only
> Pennoyer is no longer fully "good law." Do **not** apply it on an exam.

## Facts
- **Lawsuit #1:** Mitchell sued Neff in Oregon state court. Neff was notified only by **publication**, didn't appear, and Mitchell won a **default judgment**. Neff's Oregon land was then sold to satisfy the judgment and ended up with Pennoyer.
- **Lawsuit #2:** Neff returned, found Pennoyer occupying his land, and sued Pennoyer. He argued Pennoyer never got good title because the Oregon court never had jurisdiction over Neff in Lawsuit #1, so Mitchell had no right to the default judgment. Neff won at trial, and the case went to the Supreme Court.

## Issue
Did the Oregon court have **personal jurisdiction** over Neff in Lawsuit #1?

## Rule / Significance
- A territorial approach to PJ: a state's power ends at its borders.
- Four ways to get PJ: **consent, residence, physical presence in the state, or property in the forum state.** See [[Traditional Bases of Personal Jurisdiction]].
- Three types of jurisdiction: [[In Personam Jurisdiction]], [[In Rem Jurisdiction]], [[Quasi in Rem Jurisdiction]].
- Key concepts: [[Notice and Service of Process]] and [[Attachment]]. Property used as a quasi in rem basis must be attached at the outset.

## Problems
Clear but narrow; see [[Traditional Bases of Personal Jurisdiction#Problems with the Pennoyer approach]].
