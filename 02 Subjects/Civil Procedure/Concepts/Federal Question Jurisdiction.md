# Federal Question Jurisdiction (FQJ)

**Statute:** [[28 USC 1331 - Federal Question]]: "The district courts shall have original jurisdiction of all civil actions **arising under** the Constitution, laws, or treaties of the United States."

**One requirement:** the case must "arise under" federal law.
- What if P sues under a federal law? Yes, FQJ.
- What if D raises a **defense** based on federal law? **Not enough** ([[Louisville & Nashville Railroad v. Mottley|Mottley]]).

## FQJ rule
1. The federal question must appear on the face of a **well-pleaded complaint** ([[Well-Pleaded Complaint Rule]]); **and**
2. It must be **substantial** ([[Substantial Federal Question]]): important to the case itself **and** to the federal system as a whole.

## Skipping the analysis
If **federal law creates the cause of action** (enables the lawsuit), there is a federal question. Examples:
- [[42 USC 1983]]: suits against state and local officials for violating constitutional or federal rights
- [[Age Discrimination in Employment Act]]: protects workers aged 40 and older

## Federal law vs. state law
Federal laws (passed by Congress, codified in the U.S.C.) are usually something P sues under, but can be invoked as a defense (e.g., "I can't pay under the contract because it would violate the federal securities fraud statute"). That defense does **not** create FQJ.
