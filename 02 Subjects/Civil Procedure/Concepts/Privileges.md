# Privileges

A privilege is the legal right (sometimes the obligation) to **refuse to disclose**. **All privileges can be waived.**

| Privilege | Key points |
|---|---|
| **Attorney-client** | Confidential communications to obtain or give legal advice. Covers initial consultations even if the attorney isn't hired. The **client** holds it, and it survives the client's death. |
| **Clergy-penitent** | Confidential communications to a spiritual adviser for absolution, forgiveness, or advice. Usually held by the layperson. |
| **Doctor-patient** | Communications for treatment or diagnosis. Doesn't cover what a layperson could observe (appearance, clothing, treatment dates). The **patient** holds it. **Waived when the condition is at issue** (e.g., claiming damages for distress). Federal courts limit mental-health coverage to psychiatrists, psychologists, and licensed social workers. |
| **Spousal (marital communications)** | Confidential communications during the marriage, made in reliance on marital intimacy. Either spouse controls it, and it **survives divorce**. Doesn't cover pre-marriage communications. Separate from **spousal immunity** (not being forced to testify against a spouse), which applies only during the marriage. |
| **Fifth Amendment** | Can refuse if the answer could be **criminally** incriminating. Doesn't protect against civil liability or mere embarrassment. Belongs to the individual. |
| **Attorney work product** | See [[Work Product Doctrine]]. Qualified protection, overcome by substantial need plus undue hardship. |

> [!warning] Crime-fraud exception
> Attorney-client, clergy-penitent, and doctor-patient communications are **not** protected if made to further a plan to commit a crime.
